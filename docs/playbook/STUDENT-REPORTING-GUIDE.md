# Student Reporting Guide

Write an article that helps someone understand a real part of their community.
Your job is to notice, ask, check, and explain. Strong writing starts with reliable reporting.

This guide is for a capable eighth-grade writer, including someone reading and writing at a tenth-grade level.
It assumes you can handle complex ideas but are new to interviewing and publishing.
Use it at your own pace. The worksheets support your judgment; they do not replace it.

Use sections 1–3 before the visit, sections 4–5 during the interview, and sections 6–9 afterward.
Section 8 shows how evidence becomes a finished article.

## 1. Choose a question worth answering

A subject is what you cover. An **angle** is the particular question your article answers.
You do not need a dramatic conflict. A process, change, or local connection can support a useful article.

| Subject | Too broad | A question you can investigate |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Store | Everything about the store | How does this repair shop decide what it can fix? |
| Art gallery | Art in town | How does the gallery choose and arrange one exhibition? |
| Farm | Life on a farm | How does the farm decide what reaches its stand each week? |
| Sports team | The team's whole history | How does a new player learn the team's practice routine? |

Treat your first angle as a question, not a conclusion to prove.
Change it if the evidence points somewhere more useful.
For a first article, choose one place, one main question, and a visit you can arrange with an adult.
Start with a 250–400-word Quick Take. Expand when the evidence and subject justify more detail.

### Divide the work

| Student reporter | Adult editor |
| --- | --- |
| Chooses the question and researches the subject | Helps assess access and scope |
| Prepares questions and leads the interview | Handles arrangements and accompanies visits |
| Takes notes and identifies facts needing checks | Handles permission, privacy, and sensitive issues |
| Drafts, checks, and revises the article | Reviews evidence, gives feedback, and approves publication |
| Explains what each source supports | Handles metadata, technical checks, and publishing |

The editor should ask questions that improve your decisions and preserve your voice.
You should be able to explain every sentence in the finished article and show its evidence.

### Plan four work sessions

1. **Research and preparation:** choose an angle, inspect sources, and prepare six to eight questions.
2. **Visit and interview:** allow about 20–30 minutes for the interview, plus agreed observation time.
3. **Evidence and draft:** organize notes, check gaps, and write a complete first draft.
4. **Review and revision:** work through the editor's questions, confirm changing details, and prepare publication.

Scheduling and follow-up can take several days. An unanswered question is a reason to follow up, not invent an answer.

## 2. Research before you contact someone

Begin with the subject's official website, then look for relevant independent information.
Open the actual page; a search summary can omit context or show outdated details.
Record the date you checked it and the exact location of useful information.

A **primary source** has direct knowledge: an interview, an original record, or something you observed.
An **independent source** does not represent the subject's interests.
A store's website is primary evidence for its announced hours, but it is not independent evidence of its quality.

| Information | Useful starting point | What to check |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Names, roles, and current programs | Organization website and the person involved | Spelling, current role, date |
| Hours, prices, and visitor access | Current official page, notice, or responsible staff member | Whether the information still applies |
| Founding dates and historical changes | Archives, dated records, contemporary news | Whether sources describe the same event |
| Art materials and working methods | Artist interview, artwork label, demonstration | What you saw versus what someone explained |
| Farm seasons and products | Grower interview, dated crop plan, visit | Seasonal limits; where products were grown |
| Team results and participation | League records, team information, coach | Season, age group, competition, current rules |

Look for different evidence, not a target number of tabs.
Two websites copying the same announcement count as one account.
Keep opinions and promotional claims separate from verified facts.
The [research rules](REWRITE-PIPELINE.md#stage-1-research) explain when independent corroboration is needed.

## 3. Arrange the interview and prepare the visit

Ask the adult editor to review your request and handle or join the contact.
Explain that the article is for a public website, not only a school assignment.
Request a specific amount of time and explain the topic.
Offer suitable times, respect busy periods, and accept a refusal.

### Interview request

Replace the brackets before sending. Use an adult-managed contact address.

```text
Subject: Student interview request for LagunaBeach.md

Hello [name],

I am [public name], an eighth-grade student reporting for LagunaBeach.md.
I am researching [specific question] for an article on the public website.

Could I interview you for 20–30 minutes about [topic]?
I would also like to observe [activity or object], if that is possible.
My parent or adult editor, [name], will accompany me.

Would [time option] or [time option] work? We can adjust to your schedule.
We will ask separately before recording or taking photographs.
Participation does not guarantee publication or a favorable article.

Thank you,
[public name]
[adult contact]
```

If there is no answer, send one brief follow-up after several days.
Then discuss another contact with your editor.

### Before leaving

- Confirm the person, time, meeting place, visit length, and adult attending.
- Bring your question list, notebook, two pens, and a charged device if recording is agreed.
- Check access arrangements and any restrictions on photography or observation.
- Ask the host what clothing or footwear the visit requires.
- Let staff direct access around animals, tools, machinery, artworks, and sports activities.
- Keep personal addresses, school schedules, and private contact details out of public material.

The adult accompanies visits and handles unexpected changes to the meeting location or activity.
Pause an interview if someone becomes uncomfortable or the situation changes.

### Agree on permission before recording

For this project, obtain everyone's explicit permission before recording any interview, including a phone or video call.
Ask before starting the device, note the answer, and reconfirm after recording begins.
If someone declines, take written notes with their agreement. Stop recording if permission is withdrawn.
This is our reporting policy; the adult handles any legal questions using the
[Reporters Committee's California recording guide](https://www.rcfp.org/reporters-recording-guide/california/).

Permission to record for accuracy does not include permission to publish the audio.
Ask separately about publishing photographs, video, or audio excerpts.
At a gallery, ask about reproducing the artwork itself as well as photographing people.
The editor checks the applicable permission or license before using supplied images.

For interviews or identifiable photographs of other children, obtain the child's agreement and parent or guardian permission.
A coach's permission does not replace either. These are project requirements for student reporting.
Let the editor handle sensitive personal information and decisions about public names.

## 4. Conduct the interview

Start with a clear introduction. **On the record** means information can be published with the speaker's agreed name and role.
Explain that meaning instead of assuming the person knows the term.
Do not promise anonymity or confidentiality on your own.

### Opening script

```text
Thank you for meeting with us. I am researching [question] for LagunaBeach.md.
The article may appear publicly online.
May I use your answers and quote you by name in the article?
How should I spell your name and describe your role?

May I record our conversation so I can check my notes?
The recording will stay private with our editor unless we agree separately to publish an excerpt.
You can skip a question or ask us to pause.
```

Wait for answers before proceeding. Record the agreed name, role, and permissions in your notes.
If someone wants to speak off the record, pause and involve the editor before they continue.
Do not publish material subject to an unresolved agreement.

### Ask for explanations and examples

An **open question** invites an explanation. A closed question confirms a particular detail.
Use both, but do not build the whole interview around yes-or-no answers.
Avoid leading questions that suggest the answer you want.

```text
Leading: Why is your gallery the best in town?
Open: How do you decide which artists to exhibit?
Follow-up: Can you walk me through one recent decision?
Evidence: Is there a record or example we can examine?
Confirmation: Was that exhibition held in March or April?
```

Ask one question at a time. Listen to the whole answer before moving on.
Allow a short silence; people often add the most useful detail after their first answer.
Follow an interesting answer instead of treating your list as a script.

Useful follow-ups:

```text
Can you give me a specific example?
What happened next?
What does that term mean in this setting?
Could you show me how that works?
How do you know that number?
Is that a count, an estimate, or your impression?
Has that changed over time?
What is difficult about that approach?
Who else could help me understand this?
```

### Observe and take notes

Write down concrete actions, objects, posted information, and times.
Separate what you saw from what someone told you and what you inferred.
A crowded room during one visit does not prove that a gallery is always popular.
Do not describe thoughts, feelings, smells, or sounds you did not actually establish.

Keep taking brief notes when recording. Note timestamps beside important answers.
Put quotation marks only around words you captured exactly.
Mark uncertain wording immediately; do not reconstruct a quotation from memory later.

```text
Interview ID:
Date, time, place, and method:
Reporter and adult editor:
Speaker's confirmed name and role:
Permission to quote by name:
Permission to record:
Separate permission for publishing images or audio:

QUOTE, exact words:
Recording timestamp or notebook page:
Context and question asked:

PARAPHRASE, my summary of the answer:
Timestamp or page:

OBSERVATION, what I personally saw or heard:
Time and place:

CHECK, a claim needing evidence:
Possible source or follow-up:

Restrictions or unresolved permission questions:
```

### Close the interview

Check spellings, roles, dates, and any unclear quotation before leaving.
Ask these closing questions and thank the person for their time.

```text
What have I not asked that would help readers understand this?
Is there a document, object, or person I should check next?
What should a first-time visitor or participant know?
May our editor contact you to confirm specific facts or quotations?
```

Do not promise the subject control over the article.
You can confirm accuracy without giving someone approval over your angle or conclusions.

## 5. Questions by subject

Choose questions that fit your angle. Do not ask every question below.
Research facts already available, then use the interview to understand decisions, processes, and experiences.

### Store

```text
What problem do customers most often bring to you?
Can you show me how you handle one typical request?
How do you decide what to stock, make, repair, or refuse?
What has changed about your work since you started here?
What does this location change about how you operate?
What is difficult about running this store?
What should a first-time customer know before visiting?
```

Observe a process or object with permission. Confirm current hours and prices separately.
Treat an owner's claim about being the oldest or best as a claim to investigate.

### Art gallery

```text
What connects the works in this exhibition?
How did you choose one particular work for the show?
Could you explain a material or technique in that work?
How does the arrangement change what visitors notice?
How do you work with artists connected to this area?
What do visitors often misunderstand?
What should someone visiting a gallery for the first time know?
```

Record artwork titles, artists, materials, and exhibition dates from labels or confirmed records.
Distinguish your interpretation from the artist's explanation. Ask before touching or photographing anything.

### Farm

```text
What is being grown or harvested now, and why?
Can you explain one crop's path from planting to sale?
How do weather, water, or soil affect that decision?
What happens when a crop does not develop as expected?
Which products sold here are grown here, and which come from elsewhere?
What is one task visitors rarely see?
When and how can the public visit or buy produce?
```

Confirm seasons, units, and whether quantities are estimates.
Check the scope of certification claims such as organic with the editor.
Follow the host's directions around animals, equipment, and planted areas.

### Sports team

```text
What does a typical practice include, and why?
How does a new player learn the routine?
Can you show me a drill and explain its purpose?
What has the team worked to improve this season?
How do players describe their experience compared with the coach?
What costs, equipment, or eligibility rules affect joining?
Where can families find the current schedule and registration information?
```

Check results against the relevant league and season.
Seek a player's perspective with the required permissions.
Leave private health information and personal contact details out of a routine team profile.

## 6. Organize and check your evidence

Expand your notes soon after the visit while the context is fresh.
Label remembered details as later recollections; do not turn them into exact quotations.
Keep the original notes unchanged and save any transcript as a separate working copy.

Assign source IDs, such as I1 for an interview, O1 for an observation, and W1 for a web page.
Use the same IDs in your draft notes and claim log.
The editor retains private records outside the repository, with access limited to the reporting team.
Keep them available for checking and corrections after publication.

### Claim log

Copy one row per claim. Split a sentence if it contains several facts.

```text
Claim:
Type: verified fact / attributed account / opinion / observation
Source ID and exact page, passage, or timestamp:
Date and scope:
Independent corroboration, when needed:
Status: supported / needs follow-up / omit
How the article will attribute or cite it:
Editor check:
```

An opinion can be accurately reported without being established as fact.
A farmer's estimate should remain an attributed estimate unless you verify it independently.
Attribution does not make an unsupported accusation suitable for publication.
Bring allegations or serious disputes to the adult editor before drafting them for publication.

If sources disagree, identify the exact difference and seek better evidence.
An older article on this site can be wrong. Report the conflict to the editor rather than copying it for consistency.
Use the [Fact-check Pipeline](FACTCHECK-PIPELINE.md) when a claim needs deeper review.

### Follow-up message

Use this for specific checks. A failure to reply is not confirmation.

```text
Hello [name],

Thank you for speaking with us on [date]. I am checking these details:

1. [Specific fact and your question about it.]
2. [Exact quotation from your notes, with its context.]
3. [Unresolved question or request for a supporting record.]

Please tell us if I have misunderstood anything.
We are checking accuracy; our editor will make the publication decisions.

Thank you,
[public name and adult contact]
```

If someone supplies different wording, label it as a later statement in your records.
Do not silently replace the original quotation and claim it was spoken during the first interview.

## 7. Turn the evidence into an article

First answer your main question in one sentence using only supported material.
Then choose the few details that explain the answer best.
An interview transcript is raw material. The article should organize what the reader needs to understand.

### Draft outline

```text
Working title: a plain, identifying name
Main answer: what the reporting established

Opening: identify the subject and give a concrete, useful detail
At a glance: one different practical takeaway
Section 1: explain the main process, feature, or change
Section 2: add evidence, a useful quote, or a relevant limitation
Visiting or participating: verified practical information
References: sources for facts, quotes, and observations

What remains uncertain:
What I left out and why:
```

Write the first draft without trying to perfect every sentence.
Attach source IDs as you work so you can trace each claim.
Then revise for order, clarity, precision, and repetition.
Use one main idea per paragraph and explain unfamiliar terms when they first appear.

Use quotations when the speaker's wording adds meaning or character.
Paraphrase routine explanations accurately, with attribution. Never polish someone's words inside quotation marks.
One precise detail can do more work than several adjectives.

Stay fair without manufacturing a controversy. Explain real limits that matter to readers.
Do not turn a friendly interview into advertising or let gifts determine coverage.
Tell your editor about friendships, family connections, team membership, or anything offered during the visit.
The editor decides what needs disclosure and whether another reporter should check the work.

For the finished structure and citations, use [Article Playbook §4](ARTICLE-PLAYBOOK.md#4-structure).
For interviews without URLs, use its [original reporting format](ARTICLE-PLAYBOOK.md#original-reporting-without-a-public-url).
You can draft in an ordinary document; the editor handles Markdown and metadata.

### Using AI without losing the reporting

You may use AI to suggest questions, identify unclear passages, or compare an outline with your notes.
Check every suggestion yourself. AI output is not evidence and must not supply missing facts or quotations.
Do not upload private notes, recordings, or personal details without the editor's approval and appropriate source permission.
Check automated transcripts against the original recording. Keep the writing and editorial decisions accountable to people.

## 8. Worked example: from notes to article

**Everything in this example is fictional**, including the people, place, date, records, and quotation.
It demonstrates reporting decisions. Do not publish it as a real local article.

### The reporting packet

Main question: How does Example Cycle Shop decide what work a bicycle needs?

| ID | Fictional record | What it supports |
| --- | --- | --- |
| I1 | Rowan Lee, owner, interview by Alex Chen on September 12, 2026; notes pages 1–3 | Lee asks about bicycle use and problems before proposing repairs. Written estimates separate parts and labor. No work starts before approval. Customers can decline. Lee says damage and unavailable parts can limit repairs. |
| I1, page 2 | Exact quotation in the fictional interview notes | “The first tool I use is a question.” |
| O1 | Alex's observation notes, same visit | Lee asked a customer when a clicking sound occurred. The customer pointed toward the pedals. Lee wrote on a card, then rolled the bicycle to a stand. Four other bicycles carried collection tags. |
| D1 | Printed service card, dated September 2026, viewed during the visit | Saturday assessment hours are 10 a.m.–noon. Bring the bicycle. Parts availability affects completion time. |
| I1, page 3 | Owner's additional claim | Lee said the shop was the town's oldest repair business; no supporting record was supplied. |

Editorial decisions: explain the assessment process, use the observed exchange as the opening, and attribute Lee's working methods.
Omit the oldest-business claim because it was not verified.
The four tagged bicycles do not establish how busy the shop usually is, so that detail is unnecessary.

### Outline from those notes

```text
Title: Example Cycle Shop
Opening: observed conversation about a clicking sound, O1
At a glance: assessment does not promise same-day completion, D1
Section 1: questions come before the proposed repair, I1 and O1
Section 2: estimates and repair limits, I1
Visiting: Saturday hours and what to bring, D1
References: descriptive notes for I1, O1, and D1
```

### Finished draft

This example has about 280 words before its references, within the Quick Take range.
For a real article, the editor moves the title into frontmatter instead of repeating it in the body.

```markdown
# Example Cycle Shop

At Example Cycle Shop, a repair conversation started with a sound. During a September 12 visit, owner Rowan Lee asked a customer when a clicking noise occurred. The customer pointed toward the pedals. Lee wrote on a card before rolling the bicycle to a repair stand.[^1]

> **At a glance:** A Saturday assessment does not guarantee a completed repair that day. The shop's service card says parts availability affects completion time.[^3]

## Questions before tools

Lee says the first step is understanding how someone uses their bicycle and what has gone wrong. That information helps shape the proposed work.[^2]

“The first tool I use is a question,” Lee said.[^2]

During the observed exchange, the customer's description came before the bicycle reached the stand. That sequence shows how one assessment began; a single visit cannot establish how every repair proceeds.[^1]

## Deciding what to repair

According to Lee, customers receive a written estimate separating parts from labor before work begins. They can approve the proposal or decline it. The estimate gives the customer a decision to make before the shop starts the repair.[^2]

Lee also describes limits to what the shop can do. Damage can prevent a repair, and replacement parts are not always available. Those are the owner's explanations of the shop's process, rather than a promise that every bicycle can return to use.[^2]

## Visiting

The service card viewed during the visit lists Saturday assessments from 10 a.m. to noon and asks customers to bring their bicycles. It also warns that parts availability affects completion time. These details reflect the September 2026 card; confirm the current assessment hours with the shop before visiting.[^3]

## References

[^1]: Alex Chen, observation at Example Cycle Shop, 2026-09-12. Notes O1 document the customer exchange; retained privately by the editor.
[^2]: Rowan Lee, owner, interview by Alex Chen, 2026-09-12, in person. Notes I1, pages 1–3, document the quotation, estimates, and repair limits; retained privately by the editor.
[^3]: Example Cycle Shop, printed service card dated September 2026, inspected by Alex Chen on 2026-09-12. Record D1 supports assessment hours and completion limits; retained privately by the editor.
```

The draft distinguishes observation from the owner's account and the printed card.
It does not invent a founding date, claim a successful repair, or include the unsupported superlative.
Each footnote identifies inspectable evidence without publishing private files.

## 9. Review, publish, and correct

Read the draft aloud, then complete this checklist with your editor.

```text
[ ] The article answers one clear question that matters locally.
[ ] Every factual claim has evidence in the claim log.
[ ] Facts, opinions, recollections, and observations remain distinct.
[ ] Quotations match the original record and preserve the speaker's meaning.
[ ] Names, roles, dates, numbers, and units are checked.
[ ] Changing details were checked near publication and dated where useful.
[ ] Conflicting or unsupported claims are resolved, limited accurately, or removed.
[ ] The opening contains a concrete detail, and the article avoids advertising language.
[ ] Interview and media permissions cover the intended publication.
[ ] Captions, photographer credits, artwork details, and image rights are checked.
[ ] Private contact details and unapproved personal information are excluded.
[ ] The public byline is agreed with the student and parent or guardian.
[ ] Sources are cited; private records remain outside the repository.
[ ] The adult editor has reviewed the draft and its evidence.
```

The editor follows the [Write/Rewrite Pipeline](REWRITE-PIPELINE.md) for final article checks,
metadata, rendering, and repository review. A successful build does not verify the reporting.
Before publishing images, the editor checks for private information in the image and its embedded metadata.

After publication, the editor can share the article with participants.
If someone identifies an error, compare the claim with the retained evidence and investigate.
Correct confirmed errors promptly. Explain substantive corrections to readers rather than silently changing what happened.
Keep a dated record of the correction and its supporting evidence.

The [Society of Professional Journalists' Code of Ethics](https://www.spj.org/spj-code-of-ethics/)
provides further reading on verification, fair treatment, independence, and corrections.
Use the editor to discuss difficult cases; strong reporting includes knowing when more evidence or judgment is needed.
